Steps 0–3 — Stability of the Outer Catenoid

Full derivation with explanations | Outer Catenoid Stability

Basics

1. Minimal surface

A surface whose mean curvature vanishes everywhere:

$$ H = 0 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; A'(0) = 0 \text{ for every wiggle} $$

Equivalent: a critical point of area (slope = 0). Physical realization: a soap film — surface tension forces $H=0$.

Warning: "minimal" just means critical. It does NOT automatically mean "lowest area". Could be a local min, local max, or saddle.

2. Inner vs outer catenoid

Setup. Two wire rings, same radius $r$, separated vertically by $2d$. Dip in soap. A film forms between them.

Film shape. The film is always a catenoid:

$$ f(z) = R\cosh(z/R) $$

$R$ is the radius at the narrowest point (the "neck" at $z=0$).

Boundary condition. The film must touch the rings at $z=\pm d$:

$$ R\cosh(d/R) \;=\; r $$

Why two solutions? Look at the left-hand side $R\cosh(d/R)$ as $R$ varies:

$$ \begin{array}{c|l} R \text{ small} & \cosh(d/R) \text{ huge} \;\Rightarrow\; R\cosh(d/R) \text{ big} \\ R \text{ large} & \cosh(d/R) \to 1 \text{ but } R \text{ big} \;\Rightarrow\; R\cosh(d/R) \text{ big} \\ R \text{ in between} & \text{has a MINIMUM somewhere} \end{array} $$

So $R\cosh(d/R)$ is a U-shape as a function of $R$. The horizontal line $y = r$ cuts the U twice:

Two intersection points → two values of $R$ → two catenoids. The small-$R$ intersection is the inner (narrow neck); the large-$R$ intersection is the outer (wide neck).

Key point: same two rings, two different soap-film shapes. Both satisfy $R\cosh(d/R)=r$, both have $H=0$. They just pass through the "neck radius" at two different values of $R$.

In practice: only the outer one actually appears (the inner one is unstable — any tiny jiggle collapses it). Our proof shows why: $A''(0) \geq 0$ for outer, $A''(0) < 0$ for some wiggle of the inner.

3. What "stable" means

Poke the surface. Define $A(t)$ = area after poke of size $t$.

$$ A(t) - A(0) \;\approx\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,t^2\,A''(0) \quad (\text{since } A'(0)=0) $$

Soap wants least area, so:

$$ \begin{array}{c|c} A''(0) \geq 0 & \text{poke area} \geq A(0) \;\Rightarrow\; \text{soap pulls back} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{STABLE} \\ A''(0) < 0 & \text{poke area} < A(0) \;\Rightarrow\; \text{soap escapes} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{UNSTABLE} \end{array} $$
$$ \text{minimal surface}\;(H=0) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; A'(0) = 0 $$ $$ \text{stable} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; A'(0) = 0 \;\textbf{AND}\; A''(0) \geq 0 \;\text{for every poke} $$

Minimal alone is not enough — we also need the second-derivative test. That's exactly what Step 0 computes.

Step 0 — Second Variation Formula

1. Goal — what does "stable" mean?

Poke the catenoid. Does the poked shape have more or less area than the original?

$$ \begin{array}{c|c} \text{poked area} > \text{original} & \Rightarrow\; \text{STABLE (springs back)} \\ \text{poked area} < \text{original} & \Rightarrow\; \text{UNSTABLE (keeps going)} \end{array} $$

(Soap wants less area. More-area poke → soap pulls BACK to original. Less-area poke → soap keeps ESCAPING.)

Taylor gives the sign. Let $A(t)$ = area after poke of size $t$:

$$ A(t) \;=\; A(0) \;+\; t\,A'(0) \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,t^2\,A''(0) \;+\; \cdots $$
$$ A'(0) = 0 \quad\text{(catenoid is minimal)} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A(t) - A(0) \;\approx\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,t^2\,A''(0) $$

$t^2 \geq 0$ always, so:

$$ \operatorname{sign}\bigl(A(t) - A(0)\bigr) \;=\; \operatorname{sign}\bigl(A''(0)\bigr) $$
$$ \text{stable} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; A''(0) \geq 0 \;\text{ for every poke} $$

Step 0's job: compute $A''(0)$.

2. Area Formula for Surfaces of Revolution

For a surface of revolution with profile $f(z)$ rotated around the $z$-axis:

$$ \text{Area} = 2\pi \int_{-d}^{d} f(z)\,\sqrt{1 + f'(z)^2}\; dz $$
Where does this formula come from?

Slice the catenoid into thin rings. Each ring is a tiny rectangle when unrolled:

① Width = circle perimeter:

$$ \text{width} = 2\pi \cdot f(z) \quad\text{(perimeter of circle with radius } f(z)\text{)} $$

② Height = slant of tilted band. Why not just $dz$? Because a step $dz$ up also shifts the radius outward by $df$:

$$ f'(z) \;=\; \frac{df}{dz} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad df \;=\; f'(z)\,dz $$

(Definition of derivative, rearranged.)

Pythagoras on the right triangle with legs $dz$ and $df$:

$$ \text{slant} \;=\; \sqrt{dz^2 + df^2} \;=\; \sqrt{dz^2 + (f'(z)\,dz)^2} \;=\; \sqrt{1+f'(z)^2}\;dz $$

Ring area = width × height:

$$ dA \;=\; \underbrace{2\pi f(z)}_{①} \;\times\; \underbrace{\sqrt{1+f'(z)^2}\;dz}_{②} $$

Total = sum all rings:

$$ A \;=\; \int_{-d}^{d} dA \;=\; 2\pi\int_{-d}^{d} f(z)\,\sqrt{1+f'(z)^2}\,dz $$

For the catenoid, the profile is:

$$ f(z) = R\cosh\!\left(\frac{z}{R}\right) $$

Its derivative:

$$ f'(z) = \frac{d}{dz}\left[R\cosh\left(\frac{z}{R}\right)\right] = R \cdot \sinh\left(\frac{z}{R}\right) \cdot \frac{1}{R} = \sinh\left(\frac{z}{R}\right) $$

The square root simplifies using $1 + \sinh^2(x) = \cosh^2(x)$:

$$ \sqrt{1 + f'(z)^2} = \sqrt{1 + \sinh^2\left(\frac{z}{R}\right)} = \cosh\left(\frac{z}{R}\right) $$
Notation: Define shorthand $s(z)$ for the arc length factor: $$ s(z) \;=\; \sqrt{1 + f'(z)^2} \;=\; \cosh\left(\frac{z}{R}\right) $$

This $s$ appears throughout the Taylor expansion below.

3. Perturbation Setup

Wiggle the surface by pushing the radius at each height $z$ by amount $t \cdot \psi(z)$:

$$ f_t(z) = f(z) + t\,\psi(z) $$

Boundary conditions (the rings at $z = \pm d$ are fixed):

$$ \psi(-d) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \psi(d) = 0 $$

The perturbed area is:

$$ A(t) = 2\pi \int_{-d}^{d} (f + t\psi)\,\sqrt{1 + (f' + t\psi')^2}\; dz $$

4. Taylor Expansion — Where is A(0), A'(0), A''(0)?

Question: Why expand to $t^2$? Where are A(0) and A'(0)?

The Taylor series of $A(t)$ around $t=0$ is:

$$ A(t) = A(0) + t\,A'(0) + \frac{t^2}{2}\,A''(0) + O(t^3) $$

We expand to $t^2$ because:

  • $A(0)$ = original area (just a number, doesn't tell us about stability)
  • $A'(0) = 0$ because the catenoid is a minimal surface (critical point of area)
  • $A''(0)$ = the first non-trivial term — this determines stability!

Write the integrand as $G(t,z) = (f + t\psi) \cdot \sqrt{1 + (f' + t\psi')^2}$.

Expand $G$ in powers of $t$: $G = G_0 + t\,G_1 + t^2\,G_2 + O(t^3)$.

Then $A(t) = 2\pi \int (G_0 + tG_1 + t^2 G_2)\,dz$, so:

  • $A(0) = 2\pi \int G_0\,dz$ — the $t^0$ coefficient
  • $A'(0) = 2\pi \int G_1\,dz$ — the $t^1$ coefficient
  • $A''(0) = 2 \cdot 2\pi \int G_2\,dz$ — from the $t^2$ term in Taylor series

Step 4a: Expand the square root

Inside the square root:

$$ 1 + (f' + t\psi')^2 = 1 + f'^2 + 2tf'\psi' + t^2\psi'^2 $$

Since $s^2 = 1 + f'^2$:

$$ = s^2 + 2tf'\psi' + t^2\psi'^2 = s^2\left(1 + \frac{2tf'\psi'}{s^2} + \frac{t^2\psi'^2}{s^2}\right) $$

Factor out $s$ from the square root:

$$ \sqrt{\cdots} = s\,\sqrt{1 + \varepsilon} \quad \text{where } \varepsilon = \frac{2tf'\psi'}{s^2} + \frac{t^2\psi'^2}{s^2} $$

Taylor expand $\sqrt{1+\varepsilon} \approx 1 + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} - \frac{\varepsilon^2}{8} + \cdots$

Computing to order $t^2$:

$$ \frac{\varepsilon}{2} = \frac{tf'\psi'}{s^2} + \frac{t^2\psi'^2}{2s^2} $$
$$ \frac{\varepsilon^2}{8} = \frac{1}{8}\left(\frac{2tf'\psi'}{s^2}\right)^2 + O(t^3) = \frac{t^2f'^2\psi'^2}{2s^4} + O(t^3) $$

So:

$$ \sqrt{1+\varepsilon} \approx 1 + \frac{tf'\psi'}{s^2} + t^2\left(\frac{\psi'^2}{2s^2} - \frac{f'^2\psi'^2}{2s^4}\right) $$

The $t^2$ bracket simplifies (using $s^2 - f'^2 = 1$):

$$ \frac{\psi'^2}{2s^2} - \frac{f'^2\psi'^2}{2s^4} = \frac{\psi'^2}{2s^4}(s^2 - f'^2) = \frac{\psi'^2}{2s^4} $$

Multiply by $s$:

$$ \text{square root} = s + \frac{tf'\psi'}{s} + \frac{t^2\psi'^2}{2s^3} + O(t^3) $$

Step 4b: Multiply out $(f + t\psi) \times (\text{square root})$

$$ G = (f + t\psi)\left(s + \frac{tf'\psi'}{s} + \frac{t^2\psi'^2}{2s^3}\right) $$

Collecting powers of $t$:

$$ \begin{aligned} G_0 &= f \cdot s \\[6pt] G_1 &= f \cdot \frac{f'\psi'}{s} + \psi \cdot s = \frac{ff'\psi'}{s} + \psi s \\[6pt] G_2 &= f \cdot \frac{\psi'^2}{2s^3} + \psi \cdot \frac{f'\psi'}{s} = \frac{f\psi'^2}{2s^3} + \frac{\psi f'\psi'}{s} \end{aligned} $$

Step 4c: Read off A(0), A'(0), A''(0)

The three terms explicitly:

$A(0) = 2\pi \int_{-d}^{d} f\,s\,dz$ — the original catenoid area

$A'(0) = 2\pi \int_{-d}^{d} \left(\frac{ff'\psi'}{s} + \psi s\right)dz = 0$ — vanishes because catenoid is minimal

$A''(0) = 2\pi \int_{-d}^{d} \left(\frac{f}{s^3}\psi'^2 + \frac{2f'}{s}\psi\psi'\right)dz$ — this is what we need!

5. Substitute Catenoid Values

Plug in $f = R\cosh(z/R)$, $f' = \sinh(z/R)$, $s = \cosh(z/R)$:

First coefficient:

$$ \frac{f}{s^3} = \frac{R\cosh(z/R)}{\cosh^3(z/R)} = \frac{R}{\cosh^2(z/R)} $$

Second coefficient:

$$ \frac{f'}{s} = \frac{\sinh(z/R)}{\cosh(z/R)} = \tanh\left(\frac{z}{R}\right) $$

So:

$$ A''(0) = 2\pi \int_{-d}^{d}\left(\frac{R}{\cosh^2(z/R)}\psi'^2 + 2\tanh\left(\frac{z}{R}\right)\psi\psi'\right)dz $$

6. Integration by Parts (First Round)

The second term has $\psi\psi'$. Notice $2\psi\psi' = (\psi^2)'$ by chain rule.

So:

$$ \int_{-d}^{d} \tanh\left(\frac{z}{R}\right) \cdot (\psi^2)'\,dz $$

Integration by parts with $u = \tanh(z/R)$, $v = \psi^2$:

$$ = \left[\tanh\left(\frac{z}{R}\right) \cdot \psi^2\right]_{-d}^{d} - \int_{-d}^{d} \frac{d}{dz}\left[\tanh\left(\frac{z}{R}\right)\right] \cdot \psi^2\,dz $$

Boundary term: $\psi(\pm d) = 0$, so $[\tanh \cdot \psi^2]_{-d}^{d} = 0$.

Derivative of tanh:

$$ \frac{d}{dz}\left[\tanh\left(\frac{z}{R}\right)\right] = \frac{1}{R}\text{sech}^2\left(\frac{z}{R}\right) = \frac{1}{R\cosh^2(z/R)} $$

So the second term becomes:

$$ \int \tanh \cdot (\psi^2)' = -\int_{-d}^{d} \frac{\psi^2}{R\cosh^2(z/R)}\,dz $$

Combining both terms:

$$ A''(0) = 2\pi \int_{-d}^{d}\left(\frac{R\psi'^2}{\cosh^2(z/R)} - \frac{\psi^2}{R\cosh^2(z/R)}\right)dz $$

Factor out $1/\cosh^2$:

$$ A''(0) = 2\pi \int_{-d}^{d} \frac{1}{\cosh^2(z/R)}\left(R\psi'^2 - \frac{\psi^2}{R}\right)dz $$

7. Substitution to Simplify

The formula has $\frac{1}{\cosh^2}$ in front. To absorb this weight, define:

Substitution
$$ \phi(z) = \frac{\psi(z)}{\cosh(z/R)} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \psi(z) = \phi(z)\cosh\left(\frac{z}{R}\right) $$

Now $\psi^2 = \phi^2 \cosh^2$, so when divided by $\cosh^2$, we get $\phi^2$ — the weight is absorbed.

8. Calculations

We need to substitute $\psi = \phi\cosh(z/R)$ into the boxed formula.

Step 8a: Compute $\psi'$

By product rule:

$$ \psi' = \phi'\cosh\left(\frac{z}{R}\right) + \phi \cdot \frac{1}{R}\sinh\left(\frac{z}{R}\right) $$

Step 8b: Compute $\psi'^2$

Square it (writing $\cosh$ for $\cosh(z/R)$, $\sinh$ for $\sinh(z/R)$):

$$ \psi'^2 = \phi'^2\cosh^2 + 2\phi'\phi \cdot \frac{\cosh \cdot \sinh}{R} + \phi^2 \cdot \frac{\sinh^2}{R^2} $$

Step 8c: Compute $R\psi'^2/\cosh^2$

Divide each term by $\cosh^2$ and multiply by $R$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{R\psi'^2}{\cosh^2} &= R\phi'^2 + \frac{2\phi'\phi\sinh}{\cosh} + \frac{\phi^2\sinh^2}{R\cosh^2} \\[6pt] &= R\phi'^2 + 2\phi'\phi\tanh\left(\frac{z}{R}\right) + \frac{\phi^2\tanh^2(z/R)}{R} \end{aligned} $$

Step 8d: Compute $\psi^2/(R\cosh^2)$

$$ \frac{\psi^2}{R\cosh^2} = \frac{\phi^2\cosh^2}{R\cosh^2} = \frac{\phi^2}{R} $$

Step 8e: Subtract

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{R\psi'^2}{\cosh^2} - \frac{\psi^2}{R\cosh^2} &= R\phi'^2 + 2\phi'\phi\tanh + \frac{\phi^2\tanh^2}{R} - \frac{\phi^2}{R} \\[6pt] &= R\phi'^2 + 2\phi'\phi\tanh + \frac{\phi^2}{R}(\tanh^2 - 1) \end{aligned} $$

Using identity $\tanh^2 - 1 = -\text{sech}^2 = -1/\cosh^2$:

$$ = R\phi'^2 + 2\phi'\phi\tanh\left(\frac{z}{R}\right) - \frac{\phi^2}{R\cosh^2(z/R)} $$

So:

$$ A''(0) = 2\pi \int_{-d}^{d}\left(R\phi'^2 + 2\phi'\phi\tanh\left(\frac{z}{R}\right) - \frac{\phi^2}{R\cosh^2(z/R)}\right)dz $$

9. Final Integration by Parts

Step 9a: Handle the middle term

Again, $2\phi'\phi = (\phi^2)'$. Same IBP as before:

$$ \int \tanh\left(\frac{z}{R}\right)(\phi^2)'\,dz = \underbrace{[\tanh \cdot \phi^2]_{-d}^{d}}_{=0} - \int \frac{\phi^2}{R\cosh^2}\,dz $$

This contributes $-\int \phi^2/(R\cosh^2)\,dz$, combining with the existing $-\int \phi^2/(R\cosh^2)\,dz$:

$$ A''(0) = 2\pi \int_{-d}^{d}\left(R\phi'^2 - \frac{2\phi^2}{R\cosh^2(z/R)}\right)dz $$

Factor out $R$:

$$ A''(0) = 2\pi R \int_{-d}^{d}\left(\phi'^2 - \frac{2\phi^2}{R^2\cosh^2(z/R)}\right)dz $$

Step 9b: Convert $\phi'^2$ to get the $\int\phi(L\phi)$ form

Use IBP on $\int\phi'^2$. Let $u = \phi$, integrate $\phi''$:

$$ \int_{-d}^{d}\phi \cdot \phi''\,dz = [\phi \cdot \phi']_{-d}^{d} - \int_{-d}^{d}\phi' \cdot \phi'\,dz $$

Rearranging (boundary term = 0 since $\phi(\pm d) = 0$):

$$ \int_{-d}^{d}\phi'^2\,dz = -\int_{-d}^{d}\phi\,\phi''\,dz $$

Substitute back:

$$ A''(0) = 2\pi R \int_{-d}^{d}\left(-\phi\phi'' - \frac{2\phi^2}{R^2\cosh^2(z/R)}\right)dz $$

Factor out $\phi$:

$$ A''(0) = 2\pi R \int_{-d}^{d} \phi(z)\left[-\phi''(z) - \frac{2\phi(z)}{R^2\cosh^2(z/R)}\right]dz $$

10. Final Result: The Jacobi Operator

Second Variation Formula
$$ A''(0) = 2\pi R \int_{-d}^{d} \phi(z)\,(L\phi)(z)\,dz $$

where the Jacobi operator $L$ is:

Jacobi Operator
$$ (L\phi)(z) = -\phi''(z) - \frac{2}{R^2\cosh^2(z/R)}\,\phi(z) $$

What does $L$ do?

  • $-\phi''$: Penalizes sharp wiggles (smooth perturbations cost less area)
  • $-\frac{2}{R^2\cosh^2}\phi$: Curvature term — strongest at neck ($z=0$), encourages collapse

Stability criterion: Catenoid is stable iff $\int\phi(L\phi)\,dz \geq 0$ for all valid $\phi$.

End of Step 0

Step 1 — Solve the Jacobi ODE

1. Goal

From Step 0:

$$ A''(0) = 2\pi R \int_{-d}^{d}\phi(L\phi)\,dz, \qquad (L\phi)(z) = -\phi''(z) - \frac{2}{R^2\cosh^2(z/R)}\,\phi(z) $$

Step 1 goal: find $h$ solving

$$ Lh = 0 \quad\text{(the Jacobi equation)} $$

We need a specific $h$ here because Step 3 will use it to prove $A''(0) \geq 0$.

2. Simplify the ODE

Expand $Lh = 0$:

$$ h''(z) + \frac{2}{R^2\cosh^2(z/R)}\,h(z) = 0 $$

Let $u = z/R$, so $du/dz = 1/R$. Chain rule for the derivatives:

$$ \frac{dh}{dz} \;=\; \frac{dh}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dz} \;=\; \frac{1}{R}\,\frac{dh}{du} $$
$$ \frac{d^2 h}{dz^2} \;=\; \frac{d}{dz}\!\left(\frac{1}{R}\,\frac{dh}{du}\right) \;=\; \frac{1}{R}\cdot\frac{d^2 h}{du^2}\cdot\frac{du}{dz} \;=\; \frac{1}{R^2}\,\frac{d^2 h}{du^2} $$

Substitute and use $\cosh(z/R) = \cosh u$:

$$ \frac{1}{R^2}\,h''(u) \;+\; \frac{2}{R^2\cosh^2 u}\,h(u) \;=\; 0 $$

Multiply both sides by $R^2$:

$$ h''(u) + \frac{2}{\cosh^2 u}\,h(u) = 0 $$

Cleaner — $R$ is gone.

3. Where does $h$ come from?

What we need from this step, and why

Job of Step 1: produce ONE function $h(z)$ with $Lh = 0$.

Why we want it: Step 3 will use a clever trick (Picone's identity) to prove $A''(0) \geq 0$. That trick needs exactly one ingredient — a positive function $h$ with $Lh = 0$. Step 1's only purpose is to hand that ingredient to Step 3.

So: don't worry yet about what $Lh = 0$ means geometrically. We just need one function that satisfies it. Below we'll cheat — instead of solving the ODE, we'll exploit a geometric meaning of $Lh = 0$ to read off two solutions for free.

Geometric meaning of $Lh = 0$ — derived from Lecture 2's $H(f)$ formula

The equation $Lh = 0$ has a geometric meaning: pushing the catenoid in direction $\psi = h\cosh(z/R)$ keeps the surface minimal to first order in the push amount $t$. The lectures do not state this — we prove it here from Lecture 2's explicit formula for $H(f)$.

Lemma (Linearization of $H$ at the catenoid). Let $\psi(z)$ be smooth and put $h(z) := \psi(z)/\cosh(z/R)$. Then

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\!H(f_0 + t\psi)(z) \;=\; -\,\frac{Lh(z)}{\cosh^2(z/R)}. $$

Since $1/\cosh^2(z/R) > 0$ everywhere, the left-hand side vanishes identically iff $Lh \equiv 0$. So "$Lh = 0$" really is "$f_0 + t\psi$ stays minimal to first order in $t$."

Proof. One long differentiation. Every line writes the formula out — no shorthand letters.

The formula for $H$ from Lecture 2. For any profile $f$,

$$ H(f)(z) \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{f'(z)^2 + 1}}\,\Bigg[-\frac{1}{f(z)} \;+\; \frac{f''(z)}{f'(z)^2 + 1}\Bigg]. $$

This formula has three places where $f$ appears: the $\sqrt{f'^2+1}$ on the outside, the $-1/f$ inside the bracket, and the $f''/(f'^2+1)$ inside the bracket. Replace $f$ by $f_0 + t\psi$ and take $\tfrac{d}{dt}$ at $t = 0$ — every place contributes a piece. We do them one at a time.

Derivative of the $-1/f$ piece. Like $\frac{d}{dx}(-1/x) = 1/x^2$:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\!\left(-\frac{1}{f_0(z) + t\psi(z)}\right) \;=\; \frac{\psi(z)}{f_0(z)^2} \;=\; \frac{\psi(z)}{R^2\cosh^2(z/R)}. $$

Derivative of the $\sqrt{f'^2+1}$ piece. Inside the root, the rate at $t=0$ is $2 f_0'\psi'$; the square root contributes $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\,\cdot\,}}$:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\!\sqrt{(f_0' + t\psi')^2 + 1} \;=\; \frac{f_0'(z)\,\psi'(z)}{\sqrt{f_0'(z)^2 + 1}} \;=\; \frac{\sinh(z/R)\,\psi'(z)}{\cosh(z/R)}. $$

Derivative of the $f''/(f'^2+1)$ piece. Quotient rule: top derives in $t$ to $\psi''$, bottom to $2 f_0'\psi'$:

$$ \frac{\psi''(z)}{f_0'(z)^2+1} \;-\; \frac{f_0''(z)\cdot 2 f_0'(z)\,\psi'(z)}{(f_0'(z)^2+1)^2} \;=\; \frac{\psi''(z)}{\cosh^2(z/R)} \;-\; \frac{2\sinh(z/R)\,\psi'(z)}{R\cosh^3(z/R)}. $$

Combine via the quotient rule on $H$. Write $H = N/D$ where $N = -1/f + f''/(f'^2+1)$ and $D = \sqrt{f'^2+1}$. The quotient rule gives

$$ \frac{dH}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0} \;=\; \frac{(\text{deriv. of }N)\cdot D(f_0) \;-\; N(f_0)\cdot(\text{deriv. of }D)}{D(f_0)^2}. $$

The minimality of the catenoid kills one term. At $f = f_0$:

$$ N(f_0)(z) \;=\; -\frac{1}{R\cosh(z/R)} + \frac{(1/R)\cosh(z/R)}{\cosh^2(z/R)} \;=\; -\frac{1}{R\cosh(z/R)} + \frac{1}{R\cosh(z/R)} \;=\; 0. $$

This is just "$H(f_0) = 0$", i.e. the catenoid is minimal (Lecture 2 eqn $\star$). It kills the second term. We are left with

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\!H(f_0 + t\psi) \;=\; \frac{1}{\cosh(z/R)}\Bigg[\frac{\psi(z)}{R^2\cosh^2(z/R)} + \frac{\psi''(z)}{\cosh^2(z/R)} - \frac{2\sinh(z/R)\,\psi'(z)}{R\cosh^3(z/R)}\Bigg]. \quad (\bullet) $$

Substitute $\psi(z) = h(z)\cosh(z/R)$. Use $\frac{d}{dz}\cosh(z/R) = \frac{1}{R}\sinh(z/R)$:

$$ \psi'(z) \;=\; h'(z)\cosh(z/R) \;+\; \frac{h(z)\sinh(z/R)}{R}, $$
$$ \psi''(z) \;=\; h''(z)\cosh(z/R) \;+\; \frac{2 h'(z)\sinh(z/R)}{R} \;+\; \frac{h(z)\cosh(z/R)}{R^2}. $$

Plug into $(\bullet)$ and group by $h''$, $h'$, $h$:

  • $h''$ piece comes only from $\psi''/\cosh^2$: gives $\dfrac{h''(z)\cosh(z/R)}{\cosh^2(z/R)} = \dfrac{h''(z)}{\cosh(z/R)}.$
  • $h'$ pieces cancel exactly. From $\psi''/\cosh^2$: $+\dfrac{2 h'(z)\sinh(z/R)}{R\cosh^2(z/R)}$. From $-2\sinh\psi'/(R\cosh^3)$: $-\dfrac{2 h'(z)\sinh(z/R)}{R\cosh^2(z/R)}$. Sum is $0$. ✓
  • $h$ pieces. Three contributions:
    • From $\psi/(R^2\cosh^2)$: $\;\dfrac{h(z)}{R^2\cosh(z/R)}.$
    • From $\psi''/\cosh^2$ (the $h\cosh/R^2$ piece): $\;\dfrac{h(z)}{R^2\cosh(z/R)}.$
    • From $-2\sinh\psi'/(R\cosh^3)$ (the $h\sinh/R$ piece): $\;-\dfrac{2 h(z)\sinh^2(z/R)}{R^2\cosh^3(z/R)}.$
    Add: $\dfrac{2 h(z)}{R^2\cosh^3(z/R)}\big[\cosh^2(z/R) - \sinh^2(z/R)\big] \;=\; \dfrac{2 h(z)}{R^2\cosh^3(z/R)}$, since $\cosh^2 - \sinh^2 = 1$.

So the bracket in $(\bullet)$ simplifies to $\dfrac{h''(z)}{\cosh(z/R)} + \dfrac{2 h(z)}{R^2\cosh^3(z/R)}.$

Assemble. Multiply by the outside $1/\cosh(z/R)$ and pull out a common $1/\cosh^2(z/R)$:

$$ \frac{dH}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0} \;=\; \frac{h''(z)}{\cosh^2(z/R)} + \frac{2 h(z)}{R^2\cosh^4(z/R)} \;=\; \frac{1}{\cosh^2(z/R)}\Bigg[h''(z) + \frac{2 h(z)}{R^2\cosh^2(z/R)}\Bigg]. $$

The bracket is exactly $-Lh(z)$ since $Lh = -h'' - \tfrac{2 h}{R^2\cosh^2(z/R)}.$ Therefore

$$ \frac{dH}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0} \;=\; -\,\frac{Lh(z)}{\cosh^2(z/R)}, $$

which is the lemma. ∎

The shortcut: wiggle the catenoid family, get $L\psi = 0$ for free

Now we cash in the geometric meaning. We want a $\psi$ with $L\psi = 0$. The meaning says: that's the same as a $\psi$ for which $f_0 + t\psi$ is minimal at first order in $t$.

Free lunch

If we can find a $\psi$ such that $f_0 + t\psi$ is genuinely minimal for every small $t$ (not just to first order), then in particular it's minimal at first order — so $L\psi = 0$ automatically. No ODE solving needed.

Where can we find such $\psi$? From the catenoid family itself. The general catenoid has two parameters:

$$ f(z;\, z_0,\, R) \;=\; R\cosh\!\left(\frac{z - z_0}{R}\right). $$

For every choice of $(z_0, R)$, this is minimal. So if we hold one parameter fixed and wiggle the other, we sweep out a 1-parameter family of EXACT minimal surfaces — and the tangent direction $\psi = \partial_t f_t |_{t=0}$ is a ready-made solution with $L\psi = 0$.

Two natural wiggles to try

$$ \begin{array}{l|c|c} & \textbf{Slide: } z_0 \to z_0 + t & \textbf{Scale: } R \to R + t \\ \hline \text{What it does to the catenoid} & \text{shifts it up by }t & \text{makes it fatter by }t \\ \text{Result still minimal for every }t? & \checkmark \text{ (same shape, moved)} & \checkmark \text{ (same family, different }R\text{)} \\ \text{So }Lh = 0? & \checkmark \text{ automatically} & \checkmark \text{ automatically} \\ \text{Corresponding }\psi & \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial z_0}\Big|_{z_0=0} & \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial R} \end{array} $$

Each wiggle gives a $\psi$. Then we recover $h$ by the Step 0 substitution:

$$ h(z) \;=\; \frac{\psi(z)}{\cosh(z/R)}. $$

The next two subsections compute $\psi$ and $h$ for each wiggle — just derivatives, no tricks.

Solution 1: slide the catenoid

Step 1a — new profile after sliding by $\epsilon$. Sliding the catenoid up by $\epsilon$ means: the new height-$z$ cross-section is what used to be the height-$(z+\epsilon)$ cross-section:

$$ f_{\text{new}}(z) \;=\; f(z+\epsilon) $$

Step 1b — Taylor expand to first order in $\epsilon$:

$$ f_{\text{new}}(z) \;\approx\; f(z) \;+\; \epsilon\,f'(z) $$

Step 1c — identify $\psi$. Match against Step 0's definition $f_{\text{new}}(z) = f(z) + t\,\psi(z)$ (with $t = \epsilon$):

$$ \psi(z) \;=\; f'(z) \;=\; \sinh(z/R) \;=\; \sinh u $$

(We computed $f'(z) = \sinh(z/R)$ in Step 0, Section 2. Writing $u = z/R$ for short.)

Step 1d — divide by $\cosh u$ to get $h_1$:

$$ h_1(u) \;=\; \frac{\psi(z)}{\cosh u} \;=\; \frac{\sinh u}{\cosh u} $$
$$ h_1(u) \;=\; \tanh u $$

Solution 2: scale the catenoid

Step 2a — new profile after scaling $R \to R + \epsilon$:

$$ f_{\text{new}}(z) \;=\; (R+\epsilon)\cosh\!\left(\frac{z}{R+\epsilon}\right) $$

Step 2b — Taylor expand to first order in $\epsilon$. Treat $R$ as the variable, $z$ fixed:

$$ f_{\text{new}}(z) \;\approx\; f(z) \;+\; \epsilon\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial R} $$

Product rule on $f = R\cosh(z/R)$. Two pieces:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial R} \;=\; \underbrace{\cosh\!\left(\frac{z}{R}\right)}_{\text{from } \partial R/\partial R = 1} \;+\; \underbrace{R\cdot\sinh\!\left(\frac{z}{R}\right)\cdot\left(-\frac{z}{R^2}\right)}_{\text{chain rule on } \cosh(z/R)} $$

Simplify:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial R} \;=\; \cosh\!\left(\frac{z}{R}\right) - \frac{z}{R}\sinh\!\left(\frac{z}{R}\right) \;=\; \cosh u - u\sinh u $$

Step 2c — identify $\psi$. Match against $f_{\text{new}}(z) = f(z) + t\,\psi(z)$:

$$ \psi(z) \;=\; \cosh u - u\sinh u $$

Step 2d — divide by $\cosh u$ to get $h_2$:

$$ h_2(u) \;=\; \frac{\cosh u - u\sinh u}{\cosh u} \;=\; 1 - u\cdot\frac{\sinh u}{\cosh u} $$
$$ h_2(u) \;=\; 1 - u\tanh u $$

4. Verify

Plug each into $h'' + (2/\cosh^2 u)\,h = 0$.

$h_1 = \tanh u$:

$$ h_1'' = -\frac{2\tanh u}{\cosh^2 u} \;\Rightarrow\; h_1'' + \frac{2}{\cosh^2 u}h_1 = 0 \;\checkmark $$

$h_2 = 1 - u\tanh u$:

$$ h_2'' = -\frac{2}{\cosh^2 u}\,h_2 \;\Rightarrow\; h_2'' + \frac{2}{\cosh^2 u}h_2 = 0 \;\checkmark $$

5. Pick the right one

Step 3 needs $h$ to be strictly positive on $[-d,d]$.

$$ \begin{array}{l|c|c|c} & \text{parity} & h(0) & \text{verdict} \\ \hline h_1 = \tanh u & \text{odd} & 0 & \text{zero at center — no good} \\ h_2 = 1 - u\tanh u & \text{even} & 1 & \text{positive at center — KEEP} \end{array} $$
The Jacobi field we'll use
$$ h(z) \;=\; 1 - \frac{z}{R}\tanh\!\left(\frac{z}{R}\right) $$

Satisfies $Lh = 0$, even, $h(0) = 1 > 0$. Step 2 will show $h(z) > 0$ on all of $[-d,d]$ — that's where the "outer" condition enters.

End of Step 1

Step 2 — Positivity of $h$ on $[-d, d]$

1. Goal

From Step 1 we have the Jacobi field

$$ h(z) \;=\; 1 - \frac{z}{R}\tanh\!\left(\frac{z}{R}\right), \qquad Lh = 0. $$

Step 2 asks:

Claim. When $R = R_{\text{out}}$ (the outer root of $R\cosh(d/R)=r$), $$ h(z) \;>\; 0 \quad \text{for every } z \in [-d, d]. $$

Step 3 will use this to prove $A''(0) \geq 0$ (via the Picone-type identity with weight $h$). So making $h$ strictly positive is exactly the hypothesis Step 3 needs.

2. Reduce to one variable

Let $u = z/R$. Then $z \in [-d, d] \;\Longleftrightarrow\; u \in [-d/R,\, d/R]$, and

$$ h(z) \;=\; 1 - u\tanh u \;=:\; \widetilde h(u). $$

$\widetilde h$ is even: $\widetilde h(-u) = 1 - (-u)\tanh(-u) = 1 - u\tanh u$. So it suffices to prove

$$ \widetilde h(u) > 0 \quad \text{for } u \in [0,\, d/R]. $$

3. Shape of $\widetilde h(u) = 1 - u\tanh u$

Three facts. That's it.

Fact Why
$\widetilde h(0) = 1$ plug in: $1 - 0\cdot 0 = 1$
$\widetilde h$ is strictly decreasing on $[0, \infty)$ proved below
$\widetilde h(u) \to -\infty$ for large $u$: $\tanh u \approx 1$, so $\widetilde h \approx 1 - u$
Proof that $\widetilde h$ is strictly decreasing on $[0, \infty)$

Step 1 — Compute the derivative.

Differentiate $\widetilde h(u) = 1 - u\tanh u$ using the product rule:

$$ \widetilde h'(u) \;=\; -\tanh u \;-\; u\cdot\frac{d}{du}\tanh u \;=\; -\tanh u \;-\; u\,\text{sech}^2 u. $$

Step 2 — Sign of $\widetilde h'$.

Evaluate the two pieces for $u > 0$:

  • $\tanh u > 0$ for all $u > 0$   (since $\sinh u > 0$ and $\cosh u > 0$)
  • $u\,\text{sech}^2 u > 0$ for all $u > 0$   (both factors positive)

Each piece is strictly positive, so their sum is strictly positive, so:

$$ \widetilde h'(u) \;=\; -(\text{positive}) - (\text{positive}) \;<\; 0 \quad \text{for every } u > 0. $$

(At $u = 0$ both pieces vanish, so $\widetilde h'(0) = 0$ — the graph has a horizontal tangent at the start. That's a single isolated point, not an interval, so it doesn't break strict monotonicity — see step 3.)

Step 3 — Mean Value Theorem finishes it.

Pick any $0 \leq u_1 < u_2$. MVT gives some $c \in (u_1, u_2)$ with

$$ \widetilde h(u_2) - \widetilde h(u_1) \;=\; \widetilde h'(c)\cdot (u_2 - u_1). $$

Since $c > u_1 \geq 0$, we have $c > 0$, so by Step 2, $\widetilde h'(c) < 0$. And $u_2 - u_1 > 0$. Therefore

$$ \widetilde h(u_2) - \widetilde h(u_1) \;=\; \underbrace{\widetilde h'(c)}_{<\,0}\cdot\underbrace{(u_2 - u_1)}_{>\,0} \;<\; 0 $$

i.e., $\widetilde h(u_2) < \widetilde h(u_1)$. That's exactly the definition of strictly decreasing. $\blacksquare$

Why strict monotonicity matters

Combined with $\widetilde h(0) = 1 > 0$ and $\widetilde h(u) \to -\infty$:

Unique zero crossing

By the Intermediate Value Theorem, $\widetilde h$ has at least one zero on $(0, \infty)$. By strict monotonicity, it has at most one (a strictly decreasing function can't cross any horizontal line twice). So exactly one zero — call it $u_c$.

Strict monotonicity also gives the sign rule directly:

  • For $0 \leq u < u_c$: $\widetilde h(u) > \widetilde h(u_c) = 0$, so $\widetilde h(u) > 0$.
  • For $u > u_c$: $\widetilde h(u) < \widetilde h(u_c) = 0$, so $\widetilde h(u) < 0$.

By evenness, the full sign rule on all of $\mathbb{R}$ is: $\;|u| < u_c \iff \widetilde h(u) > 0.$

Picture. Starts at $(0, 1)$ with a flat tangent, then strictly decreases, crosses zero exactly once, keeps going.

So: $\widetilde h(u) > 0$ if and only if $|u| < u_c$.

4. Naming that crossing point: $u_c$

$u_c$ = the unique positive $u$ where $\widetilde h(u) = 0$, i.e. where: $$ u_c \cdot \tanh(u_c) \;=\; 1. $$

Sign rule (the only thing you need to remember):

$$ |u| < u_c \;\Rightarrow\; \widetilde h(u) > 0 \qquad\qquad |u| > u_c \;\Rightarrow\; \widetilde h(u) < 0 $$

Translate back to $z$ using $u = z/R$:

$$ h(z) > 0 \text{ everywhere on } [-d, d] \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \frac{d}{R} < u_c $$

So the whole job of Step 2 reduces to one inequality:

$$ \boxed{\;\dfrac{d}{R_{\text{out}}} \;<\; u_c\;} $$

That's what §5 proves.

One tiny lemma we need in §5

The function $u \mapsto u\tanh u$ is strictly increasing on $(0, \infty)$. Quick check:

$$ \frac{d}{du}(u\tanh u) = \tanh u + u\,\text{sech}^2 u > 0 \text{ for } u > 0. $$

Consequence: the equation $u\tanh u = 1$ has a unique positive root, and $u > u_c \Leftrightarrow u\tanh u > 1$.

5. Why the OUTER catenoid satisfies $d/R < u_c$

Recall from §4 our goal: show $\dfrac{d}{R_{\text{out}}} < u_c$. Here's the full argument in four simple steps.

Step A — Introduce the function $g(R)$

The boundary condition of the catenoid is $R\cosh(d/R) = r$. Define

$$ g(R) \;=\; R\cosh(d/R). $$

So $R_{\text{in}}$ and $R_{\text{out}}$ are the two $R$ values where $g(R) = r$.

Step B — $g$ is U-shaped

Check the ends:

  • $R \to 0$: $\cosh(d/R)$ explodes → $g(R)$ huge
  • $R \to \infty$: $\cosh(d/R) \to 1$ but $R$ huge → $g(R)$ huge

So $g$ is huge at both ends and must dip somewhere in between. Call the bottom $R_*$.

Step C — Find $R_*$

At the bottom, $g'(R_*) = 0$. Compute:

$$ g'(R) \;=\; \cosh\!\left(\tfrac{d}{R}\right) - \tfrac{d}{R}\sinh\!\left(\tfrac{d}{R}\right). $$

Set to zero, divide both sides by $\cosh(d/R)$:

$$ \frac{d}{R_*}\,\tanh\!\left(\frac{d}{R_*}\right) \;=\; 1. $$
⚡ THE KEY OBSERVATION ⚡

Look back at §4: $u_c$ was defined by

$u_c\tanh u_c = 1.$

The equation at Step C is the same equation, just with "$u$" renamed "$d/R_*$". Since that equation has a unique positive solution, the two quantities must equal:

$\dfrac{d}{R_*} \;=\; u_c.$

One equation, two places — that's the whole trick.

Step D — Outer branch gives $d/R_{\text{out}} < u_c$

$R_{\text{out}}$ is the root to the right of the bottom, i.e. $R_{\text{out}} > R_*$.

"Bigger $R$ means smaller $d/R$" (divide a fixed $d$ by a bigger number → smaller result). So

$$ R_{\text{out}} > R_* \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{d}{R_{\text{out}}} \;<\; \frac{d}{R_*} \;=\; u_c. \quad\checkmark $$

Picture of $g(R)$

Red = inner ($R < R_*$, so $d/R > u_c$). Green = outer ($R > R_*$, so $d/R < u_c$). Outer sits to the right of the bottom, which is exactly what we need.

6. Putting it together (3-line proof)

  1. From §5: $\;\dfrac{d}{R_{\text{out}}} < u_c$.
  2. For any $z \in [-d, d]$: $\;\left|\dfrac{z}{R_{\text{out}}}\right| \leq \dfrac{d}{R_{\text{out}}} < u_c$.
  3. §4's sign rule: $|u| < u_c \implies \widetilde h(u) > 0$.
Therefore: $h(z) = \widetilde h(z/R_{\text{out}}) > 0$ for every $z \in [-d, d]$. $\blacksquare$
Step 2 Result

For the outer catenoid $R = R_{\text{out}}$, the Jacobi field $$ h(z) \;=\; 1 - \frac{z}{R_{\text{out}}}\tanh\!\left(\frac{z}{R_{\text{out}}}\right) $$ is strictly positive on $[-d, d]$.

Why does the inner catenoid fail?

Inner: $R_{\text{in}} < R_*$, so $d/R_{\text{in}} > u_c$, so $h$ crosses zero inside $[-d, d]$ — breaks the argument.

That's exactly why inner catenoids are unstable: Step 3's trick needs $h > 0$, and the inner catenoid doesn't have it.

What Step 3 will use from here

  1. $h$ from Step 1: $h(z) = 1 - (z/R)\tanh(z/R)$, with $Lh = 0$.
  2. From Step 2 (just finished): $h(z) > 0$ on $[-d, d]$.
  3. Combined via the Picone identity (Step 3 will derive this): $$ \int_{-d}^{d}\varphi(L\varphi)\,dz \;=\; \int_{-d}^{d} h^2\!\left(\frac{\varphi}{h}\right)'^{2} dz \;\geq\; 0. $$ Right side is obviously $\geq 0$ since it's a square. So left side is $\geq 0$. So $A''(0) \geq 0$. So outer catenoid is stable.

End of Step 2

Step 3 — Stability Calculation

1. Goal

From Steps 0–2 we have:

  • Step 0: $A''(0) = 2\pi R\!\int_{-d}^{d}\!\varphi(L\varphi)\,dz$, with $L\varphi = -\varphi'' - q(z)\varphi$, $q(z) = \dfrac{2}{R^2\cosh^2(z/R)}$, and $\varphi(\pm d) = 0$.
  • Step 1: $h(z) = 1 - (z/R)\tanh(z/R)$ satisfies $Lh = 0$.
  • Step 2: $R = R_{\text{out}} \implies h > 0$ on the whole closed interval $[-d,d]$.

Step 3 goal: prove that for every $\varphi \in C^2([-d,d])$ with $\varphi(\pm d) = 0$,

Step 3 Theorem
$$ \int_{-d}^{d}\!\varphi(L\varphi)\,dz \;=\; \int_{-d}^{d}\! h(z)^2\!\left(\frac{d}{dz}\frac{\varphi(z)}{h(z)}\right)^{\!2}\!dz \;\geq\; 0, $$

with strict $> 0$ whenever $\varphi \not\equiv 0$.

The right-hand side is an integral of a square (times $h^2 \geq 0$), so it is $\geq 0$ for free. The whole job is the equality.

2. $h$ vs $\varphi$

Both are smooth functions on $[-d, d]$, but they play different roles:

$$ \begin{array}{l|c|c} & \boldsymbol{\varphi}\ \textbf{(perturbation)} & \boldsymbol{h}\ \textbf{(Jacobi field)} \\ \hline Lh = 0 & \text{not required} & \text{required} \\ \text{value at }z = \pm d & \;= 0 & \;> 0 \end{array} $$

$\varphi(\pm d) = 0$ because the rings are clamped. $h(\pm d) > 0$ has no physical meaning — it's just a property we proved in Step 2.

3. Why introduce $u = \varphi/h$

  • $h$ already cancels its own $q$. Step 1 gave $Lh = 0$, i.e. $-h'' = q\,h$. We want to use that to simplify $L\varphi$.
  • Define $u := \varphi/h$. Why this is legal:
    • $\varphi$ is smooth on $[-d, d]$.
    • $h$ is smooth on $[-d, d]$ and $h > 0$ on $[-d, d]$ (Step 2).
    • Smooth $\div$ smooth-and-nonzero $=$ smooth. So $u$ is itself a smooth function.
  • Equivalent form. $\varphi = u \cdot h$. This is a re-parametrization of $\varphi$ — same information, different name. No restriction added.

4. Substitute $\varphi = u\cdot h$ into $L\varphi$

Compute $\varphi''$ by the product rule:

$$ \varphi'' \;=\; (uh)'' \;=\; (u'h + uh')' \;=\; u''h + 2\,u'h' + uh''. $$

Plug into $L\varphi = -\varphi'' - q\,\varphi$ and group:

$$ L\varphi \;=\; -u''h - 2\,u'h' - u\,h'' - q\,u\,h \;=\; -u''h - 2\,u'h' \;+\; u\bigl(-h'' - q\,h\bigr). $$

The bracketed term $-h'' - q\,h$ is exactly $Lh$, which is $0$ by Step 1. So:

$$ L\varphi \;=\; -u''h - 2\,u'h'. $$

The $q$ term is gone. (Exactly the cancellation we set up in §3.)

Multiply by $\varphi = u h$:

$$ \varphi\,L\varphi \;=\; (uh)\bigl(-u''h - 2u'h'\bigr) \;=\; -u\,u''\,h^2 \;-\; 2\,u\,u'\,h\,h'. $$

5. Recognise a total derivative

Apply the product rule to $u\,u'\,h^2$:

$$ \frac{d}{dz}\bigl(u\,u'\,h^2\bigr) \;=\; (u\,u')'\,h^2 \;+\; u\,u'\cdot(h^2)' $$
$$ \phantom{\frac{d}{dz}\bigl(u\,u'\,h^2\bigr)} \;=\; \bigl((u')^2 + u\,u''\bigr)\,h^2 \;+\; u\,u'\cdot 2\,h\,h' $$
$$ \phantom{\frac{d}{dz}\bigl(u\,u'\,h^2\bigr)} \;=\; (u')^2\,h^2 \;+\; u\,u''\,h^2 \;+\; 2\,u\,u'\,h\,h'. $$

The last two terms are exactly the negatives of what appeared in $\varphi L\varphi$. Rearranging:

$$ -u\,u''\,h^2 \;-\; 2\,u\,u'\,h\,h' \;=\; (u')^2\,h^2 \;-\; \frac{d}{dz}\bigl(u\,u'\,h^2\bigr). $$

So:

$$ \varphi\,L\varphi \;=\; (u')^2\,h^2 \;-\; \frac{d}{dz}\bigl(u\,u'\,h^2\bigr). $$

Two pieces: a non-negative one, and an exact derivative. Perfect for integration.

6. Integrate over $[-d, d]$

$$ \int_{-d}^{d}\!\varphi\,L\varphi\,dz \;=\; \int_{-d}^{d}\! h^2\,(u')^2\,dz \;-\; \bigl[u\,u'\,h^2\bigr]_{-d}^{d}. $$

Boundary term vanishes. At $z = \pm d$:

  • $\varphi(\pm d) = 0$ (admissibility, Step 0).
  • $h(\pm d) > 0$ (Step 2 — strictly, not just $\geq 0$).

So $u(\pm d) = \varphi(\pm d)/h(\pm d) = 0/(\text{positive}) = 0$, and

$$ \bigl[u\,u'\,h^2\bigr]_{-d}^{d} \;=\; 0\cdot u'(d)\cdot h(d)^2 \;-\; 0\cdot u'(-d)\cdot h(-d)^2 \;=\; 0. $$

The integral identity collapses to:

$$ \int_{-d}^{d}\!\varphi(z)\,(L\varphi)(z)\,dz \;=\; \int_{-d}^{d}\! h(z)^2\,\bigl(u'(z)\bigr)^2\,dz \;=\; \int_{-d}^{d}\! h(z)^2\!\left(\frac{d}{dz}\frac{\varphi(z)}{h(z)}\right)^{\!2}\,dz. $$

7. Result — weak stability

The right-hand integrand factors as $\underbrace{h(z)^2}_{\geq 0}\;\cdot\;\underbrace{\bigl((\varphi/h)'(z)\bigr)^2}_{\geq 0\,\text{ (a square)}}$, so the whole integrand is $\geq 0$ pointwise. Hence the integral is $\geq 0$:

Weak stability
$$ A''(0) \;=\; 2\pi R\!\int_{-d}^{d}\!\varphi(L\varphi)\,dz \;=\; 2\pi R\!\int_{-d}^{d}\! h^2\!\left(\frac{\varphi}{h}\right)'^{\!2}\,dz \;\geq\; 0. $$

This is "weak" in the sense that it allows equality. The next subsection rules out equality for nonzero $\varphi$ and upgrades to strict $> 0$.

8. Strict inequality

To upgrade $\geq 0$ to $> 0$, we show: $A''(0) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \varphi \equiv 0$. Suppose $\int h^2\bigl((\varphi/h)'\bigr)^{\!2}dz = 0$. The integrand is continuous and $\geq 0$, so it vanishes pointwise:

  1. $h^2\bigl((\varphi/h)'\bigr)^2 = 0$ on $[-d, d]$.
  2. Divide by $h^2 > 0$ (Step 2): $(\varphi/h)' = 0$.
  3. Zero derivative $\Rightarrow$ $\varphi/h = c$ constant, i.e. $\varphi = c\,h$.
  4. At the boundary: $0 = \varphi(d) = c\,h(d)$, and $h(d) > 0$ forces $c = 0$.
  5. Hence $\varphi \equiv 0$.

So the integral is zero only when $\varphi$ itself is zero. For every nontrivial admissible $\varphi$, the inequality is strict:

Step 3 Result — Strict Stability
$$ A''(0) \;=\; 2\pi R\!\int_{-d}^{d}\! h^2\!\left(\frac{\varphi}{h}\right)'^{\!2}\!dz \;>\; 0. $$

The outer catenoid is strictly stable: every nonzero fixed-boundary perturbation strictly increases area.

Summary of the proof (Steps 0–3)

  1. Step 0. $A''(0) = 2\pi R\int \varphi(L\varphi)\,dz$, with $L\varphi = -\varphi'' - q\varphi$, $q = 2/(R^2\cosh^2(z/R))$, $\varphi(\pm d) = 0$.
  2. Step 1. $h(z) = 1 - (z/R)\tanh(z/R)$ satisfies $Lh = 0$.
  3. Step 2. $h > 0$ on $[-d,d]$ when $R = R_{\text{out}}$.
  4. Step 3. Substitute $\varphi = u\,h$ where $u = \varphi/h$. The $q\,\varphi$ term is killed by $Lh = 0$, and $\varphi\,L\varphi$ collapses to $(u')^2 h^2 - (u\,u'\,h^2)'$. The total derivative integrates to a boundary term that vanishes (since $\varphi(\pm d) = 0$ and $h(\pm d) > 0$ give $u(\pm d) = 0$), leaving $\int \varphi(L\varphi)\,dz = \int h^2 (u')^2\,dz \geq 0$. Strict $> 0$ unless $\varphi \equiv 0$ (§8 contradiction argument).

Conclusion: The outer catenoid is strictly stable. ∎

End of Step 3
